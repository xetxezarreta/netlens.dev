QUIC headers & frames // RFC 9000

QUIC runs over UDP but its wire format is a stack of varints, protected headers and typed frames. This page separates the parts you can see on the wire from the parts that only exist after header protection and packet decryption.

01Packet headers

first byte · version · connection IDs

The first byte's top bit selects a long header (1) or a short header (0). Long headers carry a version and both connection IDs; short headers carry only the destination connection ID. The bottom bits are partly protected by header protection, so a passive observer cannot trust them naively.

Long header · first byte

Short header · first byte

Long packet types in QUIC v1: 00 Initial, 01 0-RTT, 10 Handshake, 11 Retry. Version 0x00000000 is a Version Negotiation packet; Retry packets have no packet number and end with a 16-byte Retry Integrity Tag.

Short header

header protection is not optional

Header protection masks the low bits of the first byte and the packet number field with a key derived from the packet's AEAD. The first byte shown here is the logical, unprotected value. On the wire, the reserved bits, key phase and packet-number length may appear different until you remove protection (RFC 9001 §5.4).

02Variable-length integers

2-bit prefix · 1, 2, 4 or 8 bytes

QUIC varints use the top two bits of the first byte as a length selector. The remaining 6, 14, 30 or 62 bits hold the value. Encoding is big-endian, and non-minimal encodings are legal although senders usually choose the shortest form.

why not protobuf varints?

different trade-off
  • QUIC's prefix is at the front, so the decoder knows the length before reading the value.
  • The length is explicit in the top two bits, not discovered by continuation bits.
  • A varint may use more bytes than necessary; the receiver accepts non-minimal forms.
  • Frame types are varints too, but all standard v1 types fit in one byte.
offsets are not varints

Packet number length is 1–4 bytes and is encoded in the short-header first byte, not as a varint. The length field of a long header is a varint.

03Frame formats

frames live inside encrypted packets

After packet protection is removed, the payload is a sequence of frames. A single packet can carry many frames; a UDP datagram can carry several coalesced packets. Frame types are varints, and most frame fields are varints or length-prefixed bytes.

06Wire notes

before you trust a frame dump
you cannot parse a protected packet by hand

The long/short headers and frame list are logical views. On the wire, header protection masks bits and the payload is AEAD-protected. A “QUIC parser” that ignores keys can only decode the invariant portion of the connection ID and version. Wireshark shows decrypted frames only when you supply the TLS secrets.

  • Version Negotiation has version 0. Its long header has no length field and the connection IDs are followed by a list of supported versions.
  • Retry has no packet number. It ends with a 16-byte Retry Integrity Tag, not a payload.
  • Packet numbers are not frame fields. They live in the packet header and are used in the AEAD nonce; frame types and stream offsets are separate.
  • STREAM data without LEN runs to the end of the packet. A parser must not assume another frame follows; the remaining bytes belong to that frame.
  • Coalescing is normal. Multiple QUIC packets can share one UDP datagram. A frame parser sees only the decrypted payload of one packet.
  • PADDING is a frame, not a byte-stuffing afterthought. It inflates packets for amplification limits and is protected like everything else.
